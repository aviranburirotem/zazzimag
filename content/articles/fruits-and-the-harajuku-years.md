If you've ever seen a photo of a Harajuku teenager in layered pastels, platform shoes and a face full of hair clips, there's a good chance it traces back to one magazine. Photographer Shoichi Aoki started FRUiTS in 1997, and for two decades it did one thing: full-length portraits of young people he found on the streets of Harajuku, with a short note on what they were wearing.

## Where the style came from

In a 2024 interview with nss magazine, Aoki placed the start of what we now call Harajuku style at around 1996. He traced its roots further back, to the 1980s boom in Japanese designer labels led by Comme des Garçons and Yohji Yamamoto, followed by a wave of interest in British designers such as Vivienne Westwood and Christopher Nemeth.

What grew out of that was less a single look than a shared language. Aoki and Tordoff describe it as community-driven: styles spread as people picked up ideas from each other on the street. Aoki contrasted it with the polished luxury of nearby Ginza and Aoyama: Harajuku was rougher, louder and more playful. Of all the substyles it produced, he named Decora, with its piles of colourful accessories, as the best known.

## The street that closed

Much of this happened on Hokoten, the pedestrian zone near Harajuku station that worked as a car-free town square for young people. Writing for i-D in 2017, Aoki described the end of the pedestrian zone in 1999 as a heavy blow. Without a car-free square where people could show off and copy each other, he argued, each new wave of Harajuku fashion came in weaker than the one before.

He also pointed to fast fashion. In his view, cheap and quick clothing raised the average standard of how people dressed, but made everyone look more alike.

## The last issue, and what came after

FRUiTS published its final monthly issue in February 2017, after 233 issues. Aoki's explanation became one of the most quoted lines in street style: "there are no more cool kids to photograph."

The photos didn't disappear. Phaidon had already collected them in two books, Fruits (2001) and Fresh Fruits (2005), and an Instagram account run by Christopher Tordoff, @fruits_magazine_archives, has introduced the archive to a new generation. In the 2024 nss interview, Aoki said he was working on bringing FRUiTS back.

> Harajuku's legacy isn't a single trend. It's the idea that a street can be a runway that belongs to the people walking on it.
